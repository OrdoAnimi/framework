# 5.5 — Knowing When It Is Working

*The Pulse is not a dashboard. It is a diagnostic.*

---

The measurement problem was named in the series that preceded this one.

Architecture functions are measured on activity. Frameworks published. Reviews completed. Workshops held. Documents approved. The measurement model rewards production. Production is visible. It can be demonstrated in a governance report. It does not measure whether a single delivery decision was better informed, faster, or more coherent because architecture was present.

Changing the measurement model is a political task before it is a technical one. The organisation that has spent a year measuring artefact production has a governance structure that validates artefact production. Changing what is measured changes what is valued. Changing what is valued changes what is defended. The Theatre Inheritance defends the measurement model that sustains it.

The political architecture built in the third article is the precondition for changing the measurement model. The mandate specifies what the architecture function is authorised to do. The measurement model should specify what success looks like when it does it. Those two documents should be consistent. In most organisations they are not. The mandate describes authority, the measurement model describes activity. The gap between them is the gap between the decision system the architecture function is supposed to be and the Theatre Inheritance it has actually become.

Closing that gap is what this article is about.

### The Dual Heartbeat

The decision system has two heartbeats. They run at different cadences. Both are necessary. When one stops, the system degrades. When both run in phase, the system is alive.

The first heartbeat is the Pulse, the operational cadence at which decisions are made, recorded, and escalated. Domain-level decisions closing in forty-eight hours. Cross-domain decisions closing in five working days. Level Four trade-offs resolved within ten working days or escalating to the CEO. When the Pulse is healthy, delivery teams know when to expect an answer and plan accordingly. When it degrades, they stop asking and start deciding for themselves. The exception rate rises, use of the approved route drops, and rework cost accumulates before anyone has named the problem.

The second heartbeat is the Refinement Cycle, the learning cadence at which the constraint infrastructure is calibrated to delivery reality. Every fourteen days. Exception log reviewed. Rework log reviewed. Flow signal read. Constraint adjustments made. It is felt not directly but in the quality of the approved route over time: whether the pre-approved options stay relevant to what delivery teams are actually deciding, or drift toward the theoretical while delivery drifts away from them.

A system with a healthy Pulse but a degraded Refinement Cycle produces fast decisions against constraints growing steadily miscalibrated. A system with a healthy Refinement Cycle but a degraded Pulse has well-calibrated constraints nobody is consulting, because the decision rhythm has broken down and delivery teams are back to deciding for themselves.

Both heartbeats running, both in phase. That is the operational definition of a functioning decision system.

### Three Metrics — Operationalised

The CIO conversation requires three numbers read from the decision log and the exception log without interpretation.

**Decision Latency.** The average elapsed time between a question raised and a binding answer produced, measured by Decision Altitude. The number the CIO sees is the trend, not the average. Latency falling for four Refinement Cycles is a system becoming more responsive. Latency rising is Architectural Decay in its earliest visible form: the system beginning to slow before anyone has named the reason. In the non-profit, Decision Latency for data domain questions has fallen from eleven days to forty-one hours over twelve Refinement Cycles. The Pulse is working.

**Deviation Rate.** The proportion of decisions requiring an exception from the approved route. A falling rate means the route is becoming more comprehensive. A stable rate means it has reached its current coverage limit, either correctly or because delivery is routing around the system rather than through it. A rising rate means either the route is losing relevance, or the environment is generating decisions the infrastructure hasn't yet absorbed. It is not a performance metric. A rate of zero isn't healthy. It means either the environment has stopped generating novel decisions, or the exception process has become invisible. Both are problems. What matters is the pattern, not the number.

**Rework Ratio.** The proportion of delivery cost attributable to decisions made without architectural routing. The most consequential metric and the hardest to calculate, because tracing rework to its originating decision needs a log that has run long enough to close the feedback loop. It is the metric that closes the political argument. Not in the abstract, where "a functioning decision system reduces rework" is a claim anyone can make, but in the specific: these three rework events last quarter trace to decisions made before the constraint existed; these two trace to decisions made after, and the Refinement Cycle has since absorbed them. The contrast is the argument. In the non-profit the calculation is still incomplete, because pre-constraint rework is still surfacing. That is the honest number. The direction is clear and the full calculation closes next quarter.

The measurement model gap is not only visible in how work gets logged. It is visible in who gets promoted. The stated model rewards delivery outcomes. The revealed model, in most organisations, rewards visibility: the person in the room when the decision gets discussed, not the person whose name is on the Decision Footprint when it goes wrong. A Rework Ratio can fall for a year while the promotion system keeps rewarding exactly the behaviour that produced the rework it replaced. The decision system doesn't fix this. It makes it visible, which is the necessary first thing, and a different thing.

A promotion decided by consensus (everyone agreed she was ready, everyone agreed he'd earned it) is the Liability Transfer Pattern wearing a different hat. The decision was collective. If it turns out wrong, the record is singular: theirs, not the room's. The organisation has simply moved the pattern from architecture into people and called it something nicer.

### What Architectural Decay Looks Like Before Anyone Names It

Architectural Decay doesn't announce itself. It accumulates in the gap between the decision system's self-description and its actual relationship to the decisions being made in delivery.

The first signal: Decision Latency rising without obvious cause. Forty-one hours becomes fifty-five, then seventy. The domain owner is still answering, the answers haven't degraded, but the routing has changed. Delivery is waiting longer, or questions are arriving more complex, or the owner is carrying more load than the design anticipated. The Refinement Cycle should catch this. If its cadence has slipped from fourteen days to "when we have time," it won't, until the rise is severe enough to hit delivery.

The second signal: use of the approved route dropping without obvious cause. Eighty percent of integration decisions through the route six months ago; sixty percent now. The deviation rate has risen, the exception log has more entries, but the Refinement Cycle hasn't absorbed them. The exceptions are handled individually rather than pattern-analysed for gaps in the route.

The third signal: the decision log becoming a compliance artefact rather than a living record. Logged because the process requires it, not consulted for pattern recognition.

When all three appear together, the decision system is in early-stage Decay. The structure looks correct, the artefacts exist, the forums meet, but the Dual Heartbeat has degraded below the threshold at which it generates signal rather than activity.

The practitioner who reads these three signals at the end of the first year catches the Decay before it becomes structural. The Refinement Cycle cadence is restored, the accumulated gaps in the route are closed in two concentrated sessions, and decision-log review becomes a standing item on the Refinement Cycle agenda. Architectural Decay caught at this stage costs a fortnight of recalibration. Caught after it's structural, it costs the organisation the decision system it took a year to build.

### The Conversation the CIO Actually Needs

Six months in, the CIO asks whether the decision system is working. The answer is a diagnostic read from three numbers and a pattern, not a presentation.

Decision Latency: eleven days to forty-one hours over twelve cycles, trending stable. Deviation Rate: eight percent, three quarters absorbed into route updates, the infrastructure learning. Rework Ratio: incomplete but directionally confirmed, full calculation next quarter. One early Decay signal, caught at cycle eleven and corrected by expanding team-level delegation for low-complexity data questions.

That is the conversation. It takes twelve minutes. It is not a governance report. It is a precise account of what the decision system is doing, what it is learning, and where the one early Decay signal was caught before it became structural.

The CIO who receives that account is not being presented to. They are being included in the diagnostic read of the system they sponsored. The Theatre Inheritance presents. The decision system reports.

The non-profit, six months in, has a CIO reading a Pulse diagnostic rather than approving a governance report. That is not a small change. It is the organisation becoming capable of a conversation the Theatre Inheritance was structurally incapable of producing.
