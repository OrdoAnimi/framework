# THE LAST SIGNAL: BACK AT THE FRIDAY TABLE

The question at the heart of this series came up on one of those late Friday afternoons when the week finally lets go. A few of us around a table, coffee going cold, asking a question most of us carried but rarely said out loud: why do so many people doing genuine enterprise-level thinking never actually hold the EA title?

Every post has been working on that question.

It went to the map first. Executing a guess. The baseline signed off six months ago and already rotting. The capability map coloured green while the man who patched the customer file most Fridays was never asked. The eighty per cent mapped that was closer to fifty.

Then to the people around the map. The engineer called onto the bridge at midnight because she had spent years in the terrain, while the EA, title and all, was never called. The concern received at every stop up the chain, because acting costs political capital and staying still doesn't. The exemption that by the fifth time had its own form. The line that closed two posts: if it is not formally in my purview, I am not responsible for knowing it exists. The architect right in March and too late by June. The same risk flagged too early, then just in time.

Twelve posts in, I said they were one mechanism in a different uniform, then stepped out to say why I write any of this down.

Then to the decisions themselves. The demo that proved it could, where nobody asked if it should. The AI-first slide sitting on an approval chain nobody touched. The team that inherited a model while the org chart stayed the same. The risk known in week nine that reached the steering committee in week fourteen.

That Friday question had the wrong subject. The title never decided anything. Three things did.

Decision Altitude: whether the call sat at the level that could carry its consequence, or was pushed up to a forum or down to someone without the full picture.

Air cover: whether anyone senior would spend political capital to stand behind whoever made it, agreed before the pushback came.

Political consensus: whether the room made the call, or agreed its way around it so the exposure was shared and the question walked out still open.

Our colleague, who never sat the formal exam, described his role as maintaining the integrity of a direction across hundreds of decisions made by people who were never in the original strategy room. That was the answer all along.

Signal was always meant to be short-term: observations from the field, a companion to the longer articles, which carry on. This is the last one.

Thank you for reading and for being part of the journey. I hope it was informative, and that a few of these rooms made your own a little easier to read.

If we sat back down at that table now, the coffee would still go cold. The question would be better.
